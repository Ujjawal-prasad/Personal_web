Adaptive Morphology for Quadrotor

Research Context: This is my current BTP at IIT Kharagpur on a quadrotor whose arm geometry can change during operation instead of remaining fixed throughout a mission.

My Work: I shaped the concept, built the simulation models, adapted the mixer and control logic, and pushed the work toward a physical prototype.

Current Status: The project has already produced stable simulated behavior across multiple configurations and an initial hardware direction, with the full technical flow available below.

Open Full BTP Technical Details

Problem Framing

The central question in this BTP is how to make a quadrotor remain controllable while its physical geometry changes. A conventional quadrotor is designed around a fixed frame, fixed arm lengths, and a fixed thrust allocation structure. In this project, the geometry itself becomes a variable, which means the controller and overall system architecture have to adapt along with the vehicle.

I am studying this through a reconfigurable quadrotor that can transition across multiple arm layouts, including X, T, and O-like configurations. The aim is to understand how morphology changes affect stability, actuation, control authority, and mission use.

What I Have Done

  • Defined the project as a complete aerial robotics system rather than only a CAD or control exercise.
  • Built the morphology concept through repeated mechanical design iterations and component-level modeling.
  • Created simulation-ready geometry and vehicle descriptions so the platform could be evaluated before hardware locking.
  • Integrated the platform into Gazebo and PX4 SITL workflows to study behavior under changing arm geometry.
  • Worked on the control allocation side so total thrust and body torques could still be meaningfully distributed as the frame changed.
  • Tested different configurations in simulation and tracked whether stable hovering could be maintained across transitions.
  • Moved the work toward hardware through an initial prototype and an electronics-level integration view.

CAD Design

The project began with CAD-level iteration on the frame, arm elements, and structural parts required for a reconfigurable quadrotor. This stage helped me reason about how the geometry could physically change before moving into simulation and control adaptation.

SDF Model Creation for Gazebo

After the CAD stage, I converted the design into simulation-oriented models for Gazebo. These figures represent the vehicle model creation stage that connected the geometry work to the simulated platform used for hover and morphology testing.

Control and Logic

The logic of the project is that changing morphology also changes the actuation geometry. That means the mapping between desired forces and motor commands cannot remain identical to the rigid-frame case. My work therefore focused on making the controller aware of the changing arm arrangement instead of assuming a constant mixer.

In practice, this meant studying how the force and moment contributions of each motor vary with arm angle, and then embedding that into the control pipeline so the vehicle can still hold attitude and altitude during different configurations. The project combines geometry reasoning, allocation logic, and simulation validation in one loop.

Swarm-SLAM Multi-Robot Simulation

Internship Context: This work was done during my internship at the Indian Institute of Science in December 2025 under Prof. Shishir N. Y., focused on making a collaborative Swarm-SLAM pipeline actually run in simulation.

My Work: My contribution was not just workspace setup, integration debugging, multi-robot launch design, namespacing, visualization fixes, and tracking the backend issue precisely enough to isolate the remaining blocker.

Outcome: The most important milestone was demonstrating real-time inter-robot loop closure in simulation, while also identifying the remaining backend issue with robot 1 keyframe optimization.

Open Full Internship Technical Details

Implementation Goal

The first target during the internship was to validate Swarm-SLAM in simulation before attempting more complex multi-robot scenarios. To keep the system manageable, I started with two robots and focused on achieving real-time inter-robot loop closure.

Setup Work Completed

  • Created a ROS 2 workspace for the Swarm-SLAM evaluation flow.
  • Used the SAM Bot from the Nav2 tutorials as the robot model in Gazebo.
  • Built and verified the robot setup before integrating the collaborative SLAM stack.
  • Cloned and built the Swarm-SLAM framework despite version warnings during setup.
  • Configured the required parameter file for the LiDAR launch flow.
  • Added the transform expected between odometry and the current pose frame.
  • Brought in the visualization package separately because it was not included by default.
  • Applied a ROS 2 timestamp fix inside the visualizer so published transforms were valid.

How the Pipeline Worked in My Setup

In my simulation setup, Swarm-SLAM consumed odometry and PointCloud2 data generated from RGB-D sensing on each robot. The frontend generated keyframes, constructed pose-graph information, and looked for loop closures. The backend then optimized the graph using decentralized logic rather than a centralized SLAM server.

The main implementation milestone was to make this flow run reliably enough that I could observe and verify an actual inter-robot loop closure in simulation instead of just launching packages independently.

Single-Robot Milestone

I first validated the framework on a single robot. That stage was important because it let me confirm basic robot spawning, sensing, visualization, teleoperation, and local map generation before moving to the more failure-prone multi-robot configuration.

Move to Two Robots

Once the single-robot flow worked, I extended it to a two-robot setup. This required more than just launching a second instance. I created a dedicated multi-robot launch flow and also prepared separate SDF copies with prefixed link and joint names so Gazebo would not confuse entities from different robots. 

Terminal 1:
ros2 launch sam_bot_description multi_robot_launch.py

Terminal 2:
ros2 launch cslam_experiments cslam_lidar.launch.py namespace:=/r0 robot_id:=0 max_nb_robots:=2 use_sim_time:=true

Terminal 3:
ros2 launch cslam_experiments cslam_lidar.launch.py namespace:=/r1 robot_id:=1 max_nb_robots:=2 use_sim_time:=true

Debugging and Observations

A large part of the work involved debugging transforms, topics, namespaces, and graph behavior. I used the pose graph and keyframe topics directly to track what the framework was doing and to understand whether both robots were contributing consistently. 

/cslam/pose_graph
/r0/cslam/keyframe_data
/r0/cslam/keyframe_odom

The main unresolved issue was that the backend optimized robot 0 keyframes but not robot 1 keyframes. Based on the observed behavior, the likely causes were anchor selection, namespace configuration, or inter-robot pose graph sharing in the decentralized backend.

Result Achieved

Even with the backend issue still open, I successfully demonstrated real-time inter-robot loop closure in simulation. In the tested scenario, one robot explored first and the second robot was then driven to the initial position of the first, at which point the loop closure was detected and the trajectories aligned.

Why Swarm-SLAM Was a Good Fit

  • It is designed to be decentralized instead of depending on one central SLAM server.
  • It supports multiple sensing modalities, including LiDAR, stereo, and RGB-D.
  • It aims to scale toward larger robot teams.
  • It reduces communication load by exchanging compact descriptors instead of full maps.
  • It performs real-time inter-robot loop closure instead of only stitching maps offline.

SKYBOT Series (SKYBOT 1 to 3)

Project Context: The SKYBOT series is my long-running journey of building custom aerial robots from scratch and learning through repeated redesign.

Progression: It started with a simple Arduino-based build, moved through failure analysis and frame redesign, and then grew into a more serious autonomy-ready drone with onboard compute and depth sensing.

Outcome: Stable SKYBOT2 flight and a continuing transition toward payload-capable and autonomy-oriented aerial systems through SKYBOT3.

Open Full SKYBOT Series Details

Where It Started

My journey with aerial vehicles began with a DJI Tello. It was the first drone I could code and experiment with, and it was also the point where curiosity turned into a serious desire to understand how drones actually work. Breaking one of its motors and waiting through the repair process pushed me to move beyond ready-made drones and try building my own.

SKYBOT 1: First Custom Build and First Big Lessons

SKYBOT 1 began as an Arduino-based attempt at building a quadrotor from scratch. At that stage I did not yet have a solid understanding of control systems, and I initially assumed the drone would simply fly once the hardware was assembled. It did not. The drone struggled even to lift properly and repeatedly toppled.

I later installed a Pixhawk flight controller and moved to PID-based closed-loop control. That got the drone airborne, but it still wobbled heavily and never became trustworthy in flight. Multiple tuning attempts and software changes did not fix the underlying hardware-control mismatch.

During development, the platform went through several failures, including damaged motors, ESCs, propellers, and frame elements. These failures were frustrating, but they were also the stage where I learned the most about how frame design, propeller choice, and motor selection affect actual flight behavior.

What SKYBOT 1 Taught Me

The key lesson from SKYBOT 1 was that stable flight is not just about adding a controller. The hardware itself has to make sense. I identified two main issues: very high KV motors are better suited to high RPM than high torque, and the large 11-inch carbon fiber propellers demanded more torque than the system could comfortably supply.

  • Use smaller propellers when the motors are optimized for high RPM rather than high torque.
  • Avoid overly rigid, crash-unfriendly propeller choices early in development.
  • Frame stress, crash survival, and component matching matter as much as control tuning.

SKYBOT 2: Redesign Toward Stability

SKYBOT 2 was built by carrying forward those lessons into a cleaner and better-matched design. This version achieved stable flight using the default PID settings, which was an important moment because it showed that the earlier instability was not only a tuning issue, but also a design issue.

From Manual Flight to Autonomy Preparation

After achieving a stable base platform, I began upgrading the drone for autonomous missions. This was the stage where the SKYBOT series moved from being purely a flight stability exercise to becoming a broader aerial robotics platform.

I integrated a Jetson Orin Nano Super and an Intel RealSense D435i depth camera so the drone could later be used for tasks such as 3D SLAM and autonomous exploration. This changed the nature of the project: the drone was no longer only about flying, but about carrying compute, sensors, and future autonomy logic onboard.

I also ran a YOLOv8 segmentation model online on the Jetson Orin Nano onboard SKYBOT 2. The video below shows that live inference setup in action on the platform.

Current Status and SKYBOT 3

The current work now extends toward SKYBOT 3, a newer build intended for small payload-carrying tasks. The exact mission use is still being shaped, but the broader direction is clear: the SKYBOT series has evolved from a first experimental build into a platform family through which I have learned flight stability, redesign, hardware integration, and mission-oriented aerial system thinking.

Work on autonomy-ready operation is still influenced by missing supporting hardware such as an optical flow sensor, but the series continues to serve as my hands-on platform for learning and iteration in aerial robotics.

Autonomous Ground Rover for Search and Rescue

Competition Context: This project was developed for the Hardware Modeling Competition 2024-25 at IIT Kharagpur around the problem of search and rescue in dangerous disaster environments.

Problem: Entering earthquake-affected or mine-collapse zones is dangerous for human responders, so the rover was designed to perform autonomous mapping while also checking for signs of trapped survivors.

My Contribution: Solely handled software architecture, mapping stack design, field-testing workflow, and integration of RealSense, Jetson, Pixhawk, motor drivers, and supporting electronics.

Outcome: The system combined SLAM with mmWave-based human-presence sensing, and field tests were carried out to validate both rover operation and survivor detection ideas.

Open Full Rover Project Details

Problem and Motivation

The rover was designed for disaster-response scenarios where sending a person directly into the environment is risky. Earthquakes, collapsed structures, and mining accidents create spaces that are unstable, cluttered, and dangerous. The goal was therefore to create a compact ground robot that could move into the area first, map the environment, and support rescue teams with useful information before human entry.

What made this project stronger was that it was not only a navigation platform. It also aimed to detect possible trapped survivors through mmWave sensing, which gave the rover a meaningful rescue-specific function beyond just locomotion.

My Role

I was solely responsible for the software architecture of the rover. That meant designing how sensing, onboard computation, mapping, control, and communication fit together as one functioning system rather than isolated modules.

  • Designed the SLAM-centered software workflow for the rover.
  • Integrated the sensing and compute stack around Jetson, RealSense, and Pixhawk.
  • Handled the data and communication paths across the rover control and sensing stack.
  • Set up and validated the mapping pipeline used during outdoor and night tests.
  • Worked on making the rover useful specifically for rescue-style deployment rather than as a generic UGV.

Why mmWave Was Important

The rover used an mmWave sensor because it can detect vital-sign-level micro-movements, such as breathing or heartbeat, even in partially obstructed environments. In a rescue setting, that matters because a trapped person may not be visible but may still be alive under debris or in a confined space.

This gave the rover a stronger rescue focus: it was not only a platform for mobility and mapping, but also an attempt to support survivor identification in conditions where direct visual confirmation may fail.

System Architecture

The rover software stack used ROS 2 Humble on the Jetson Orin Nano as the main backend. The Intel RealSense D435i supplied depth and IMU data, which were processed through librealsense2 and RTAB-Map to generate visual-inertial odometry and SLAM outputs. The Jetson worked alongside a Pixhawk 2.4.8 controller through MAVLink, while motor drivers and an Arduino were connected through Modbus and UART respectively.

From a system-design perspective, this was a full integration problem: sensing, mapping, low-level control, and rescue-oriented payload logic all had to coexist on a single rover platform.
